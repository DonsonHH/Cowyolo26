Purpose 🎯
In this project, you will work as an engineering product development team to develop a commercially viable cattle-identification product/service mix for dairy farmers in the North of England. Your solution should not simply be technically impressive. It must bring together engineering, customer needs, regulation, market positioning, sustainability, affordability and business viability.


By the end of the project, you should be able to show a clear connection between the problem you identified, the customer needs you researched, the requirements you defined, the engineering decisions you made, and the commercial model behind your solution. This assessment develops your ability to apply product-development life-cycle techniques, marketing principles and engineering management thinking to a realistic agricultural engineering problem.

Group Task 
ID-Coo Product Development & Marketing Project ✏️
“During 2026 the Livestock Information Service (LIS) is being rolled out to all cattle keepers in England, replacing BCMS as the primary system for registering cattle and reporting movements. From 2027, electronic identification (EID) [2] becomes mandatory for all new-born calves (using low-frequency technology), and paper cattle passports are set to be phased out in favour of digital records.” [1]

In a farm environment, EID may be damaged, lost or stop working. Cumulative losses of up to 19.8% [3]. This would mean that relying solely on RFID tags may fail to identify cows. Furthermore, cows sometimes bundle with each other passing through an RFID reading gate which can yield 2 IDs at once given the range for reading tags as well as miss the reading entirely.

You are to design a solution for cow identification that provides value for money for a farmer in the North of England. The target breed to begin with will be Holstein Friesian.

You are tasked to understand the market needs for cow identification in order to provide a unique selling point for your proposed solution. The characteristics of the system will require robustness in identification, reliability, and affordability i.e the price the farmers pay need to be justified by the economic benefit.

Your team will develop a Product Requirements Document and Business & Marketing Analysis


Throughout the project, you will test whether your solution responds to genuine customer needs, is meaningfully differentiated, technically feasible, sustainable, affordable for the target market and supported by a viable business model.



Learning journey
→ DISCOVER - What is the identification problem, what constraints shape it, and what does the customer need?

→ DEFINE - What value should our product/service create, and how will it be different from existing alternatives?
→ DESIGN - What must the system do, and how will our technical solution meet those requirements?

→ VALIDATE - Is the solution technically feasible, sustainable, competitive, affordable and aligned with the market?

→ DELIVER - Can we demonstrate a coherent engineering solution and a viable business model that together solve the original problem?

Read the project brief, report structure and checklist (below) carefully.


Read the Guide: Rubrics (in Cadmus Manual) and then your Marking Rubric


Identify the key constraints already built into the project. Ask yourselves: What problem are we actually being asked to solve, for whom, and within what constraints?


Divide your initial research areas, but remember that your final submission should read as one coherent product-development project, rather than separate pieces written by different people.


Step 2: Investigate the EID and LIS Context (~250 words)
Before proposing a solution, establish the regulatory and operational environment in which it will be used.


Research the constraints associated with:

Electronic Identification (EID);

the Livestock Information Service (LIS);

cattle identification and movement reporting;

relevant UK/English regulations and implementation requirements; and

the practical use of identification technology in farming environments.


Identify the most important constraints and explain what each one means for your proposed system.


Do not simply list regulations. Show their engineering or operational implications. Ask yourselves:

What does our system have to comply or integrate with?

What does the regulatory environment prevent us from doing?

What requirements does it create for our eventual design?




Write ~250 words determining the constraints for EID and regulations in relation to LIS.



TIP: Use credible sources such as government documentation, industry sources and appropriate research literature. The strongest responses explain the implications of each constraint rather than simply identifying it.



Step 3: Understand the Consequences of Misidentification (~250 words)

Now establish why the problem matters. Investigate what can happen when a dairy cow is incorrectly identified or cannot be identified reliably.


Consider consequences for:

individual animals;

day-to-day farm operations;

farm records;

LIS information;

milk-buyer information; and

other relevant management or financial processes.


Show clear cause-and-effect relationships. For example, do not stop at: “The wrong cow may be identified.” Continue by explaining: What happens because of that? Who is affected? What operational, financial, welfare or regulatory consequences follow?


Ask yourselves: Which consequences are serious enough that our product must explicitly prevent or reduce them?


Write ~250 words on the consequences of misidentification of diary cows in farm environments.



Step 4: Understand Your Customer and Formulate the Idea Value Proposition Canvas
Before deciding exactly what your product will be, investigate the people who would use, purchase or benefit from it. Use your research to develop a Value Proposition Canvas:


Identify your customer's:

Jobs — what are farmers trying to achieve when identifying and managing cattle?

Pains — what currently makes identification unreliable, difficult, costly or inefficient?

Gains — what would make identification more dependable, useful or valuable?


Then consider your proposed:

Products and services;

Pain relievers; and

Gain creators.


Your proposed solution should respond directly to the customer insight you have identified.


Create your Value Proposition Canvas as an A3 sheet or legible digital equivalent, as instructed in the report structure. Upload this to the Cadmus group work area.


Ask yourselves: Can we point to evidence for these customer needs, or are we assuming that farmers want them?



TIP: Keep returning to the connection: Customer need → Product/service response → Customer value



Step 5: Establish Your Competitive Frame of Reference (up to 500)
Before claiming that your idea is different, investigate what already exists. Identify relevant competitors and alternative ways farmers currently solve the identification problem.


For each important competitor, identify your:

Points of Parity — what must your solution offer simply to be a credible alternative?

Points of Difference — where could your solution provide meaningful additional value?


Compare competitors individually rather than making unsupported general claims about “the market”. Ask yourselves:

What would customers already expect from a product like ours?

Where are existing solutions insufficient?

Which differences would actually matter to a farmer?



TIP: A difference is only strategically useful if it creates meaningful value for the customer.

Step 6: Analyse the Competitive and Market Environment (hard limit - up to 1000 words - no images)

Deepen your analysis by selecting at least two appropriate analytical tools. These might include:

SWOT;

Porter’s Five Forces;

PESTEL;

simple market-analysis tools; or

another relevant approach supported by your learning and research.


Choose the tools that will generate the most useful insight for your particular product. You do not need to use every framework. Use your analysis to establish:

the desirable attributes your product/service mix needs;

important opportunities or constraints;

threats or competitive pressures;

and where meaningful differentiation may be possible.


Most importantly, explain what your analysis means for your product. Ask yourselves: What decision about our product, market or business model can we make because of this analysis?



TIP: Avoid completing frameworks mechanically. Analysis becomes useful when its findings influence the product you develop.



Step 7: Define Your Unique Selling Proposition (~150 words)
Bring together your customer and competitor analysis to create a concise Unique Selling Proposition (USP). 


Your USP should communicate the most important reason your target customer would choose your solution. Aim to connect: Customer need → Distinctive benefit → Reason to choose your solution


Ask yourselves: If a farmer asked, “Why should I pay for this rather than use an existing alternative?”, can we answer clearly in one sentence?



TIP: Keep your USP crisp and distinct. Imagine you only have 10 seconds to grab my attention.



Step 8: Build Your Business Model — Business Model Canvas

Now consider how the complete product/service could operate as a viable business. Complete your Business Model Canvas, considering:

customer segments;

value proposition;

channels;

customer relationships;

revenue streams;

key resources;

key activities;

key partners; and

cost structure.


Your canvas should reflect the product and customer you have already developed rather than introducing a completely new business idea.


Check the relationships between the blocks. Ask yourselves:

Who pays for the solution?

How do we reach them?

What resources and partners are necessary to deliver it?

Where does recurring revenue come from?

Does the revenue model realistically support what we are promising customers?


Upload your Business Model Canvas to the Cadmus Group Work area.



TIP: The strongest Business Model Canvases show logical connections between the blocks, rather than treating each box as an independent activity.



Step 9: Critical Analysis Test the Market and Product–Customer Fit (
Report section 8 — up to 1 page - no image


Step back and critically test the commercial assumptions you have made. Estimate:

the size of the relevant market;

what proportion of that market you could reasonably service; and

whether your proposed desirable attributes genuinely align with what your chosen customer segment values.


Then reconsider your product/service mix. Ask yourselves:

Is the product/service mix appropriate for the customer segment we selected?

If the evidence suggests that your idea needs to change, change it and explain why. Product development is an iterative process.





Step 10: Translate the Problem into User Requirements
Report section 9 — up to 1 page


Using your product/service mix idea, write down everything your product needs to do. User requirements define what is needed to solve the problem. At this stage, the user requirements outline what the product needs to do, not how the problem will be solved. (up to 10 requirements). Justify the reasoning behind them. This exercise ensures that all user requirements align with solving the problem defined at the beginning. Take each user requirement identified in the previous section, justify why they are needed and help solve the problem. Using the MOSCOW analysis framework insure that all 10 requirements are must have.




Now move from understanding the problem to defining exactly what your product needs to achieve. Develop up to 10 user requirements. Link the features to the initial problem


At this stage, focus on what the system must do, not the technical solution you will use to achieve it. For each requirement:

State the requirement clearly.

Explain which aspect of the original problem or customer need it addresses.

Justify why it is necessary.

Use the MoSCoW framework to check its priority.


For this project, your final selected requirements should represent genuine Must Have requirements. Ask yourselves: Could somebody understand what our product needs to accomplish without knowing how we intend to build it?


TIP: Avoid turning a technical solution into a requirement too early. For example, “the system must use camera X” describes how; a user requirement should describe what needs to be achieved.



Step 11: Design and Explain Your Technical Solution
Report section 10 — up to 4 pages (up to 2 pictures) 1 picture removes half a page


Now decide how your system will meet the user requirements you have defined. Develop a coherent technical description of your proposed solution. Depending on your design, this may include:

the physical structure through which cattle move;

sensor placement;

identification equipment;

electronics or circuits;

data collection and aggregation;

algorithms and/or machine learning;

software;

functional block diagrams;

interfaces between components;

integration with LIS;

the farmer/user interface; and

relevant pricing considerations.


Use diagrams, schematics and flow diagrams where they make the system easier to understand, following appropriate standard conventions.


Show clearly how information moves through the system: Animal/input → Identification → Processing → Decision/data → Interface/LIS/output


Then check your technical design against your requirements. Ask yourselves:

Which user requirement does each major component address?

How does the complete system operate from beginning to end?

Where could identification fail?

What happens when something goes wrong?

Can the different components realistically communicate with one another?

Is this solution consistent with the price point we are proposing?



TIP: A technically sophisticated design is not automatically a good design. The strongest solution is one in which the technical choices clearly solve the identified problem and remain consistent with the proposed product and business model.



Step 12: Evaluate Sustainability and Refine the Design (~250 words - no images)
Report section 11 — up to half a page (no picture)

Evaluate how your proposed solution contributes to sustainable development.


Relate your design to relevant principles from the Dairy Roadmap and support your discussion with details from your own proposed system. Consider where appropriate:

resource and energy use;

materials and components;

product lifetime;

maintenance and replacement;

waste;

efficiency improvements;

environmental impacts; and

wider sustainability implications for dairy farming.


Go beyond saying that your product is “sustainable”. Explain which aspects of the design create an impact, what trade-offs exist, and whether the design should be modified as a result of your analysis.


Ask yourselves: Has our sustainability analysis actually changed or improved any of our engineering decisions?



TIP: Critical evaluation may reveal weaknesses in your first design. Revising the design in response to evidence is a strength, not a problem.



Step 13: Report section 12 - Test Affordability (~250 words - no images)

Finally, determine whether the product/service mix offers genuine value for money for your target farmer. Estimate and justify:

installation or purchase costs;

recurring service costs; and

any other significant ongoing costs associated with the solution.


Then compare those costs with the economic value or benefit the product could provide. Ask yourselves:

What does it realistically cost us to provide this solution?

What would the farmer have to pay?

What economic benefit could justify that expenditure?

Does our price remain consistent with the technical system and Business Model Canvas we have proposed?


TIP: Do not simply choose an attractive price and work backwards. Your affordability argument should connect: Cost to deliver → Price to customer → Economic benefit → Value for money
